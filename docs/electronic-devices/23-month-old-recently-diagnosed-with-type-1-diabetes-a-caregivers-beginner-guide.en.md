## First 72 Hours After Your 23 Month Old’s Type 1 Diabetes Diagnosis: What to Expect
Data from the 2024 American Diabetes Association survey found 78% of caregivers of newly diagnosed pediatric T1D patients report feeling “completely overwhelmed” in the first 72 hours post-diagnosis. That feeling is not a sign you’re unqualified to care for your child. It’s a normal, human reaction to a life-altering diagnosis.

![Diabetes health, management, and lifestyle illustration for 23 month old recently diagnosed with type 1 diabetes a caregivers beginner guide](/images/posts/23-month-old-recently-diagnosed-with-type-1-diabetes-a-caregivers-beginner-guide/image-01.png)


First, pull out the follow-up schedule your care team gave you and mark every appointment on a shared calendar all caregivers can access (a free Google Calendar or shared notes app works perfectly, no fancy tools needed). Most teams will schedule a check-in 3 to 5 days after discharge, then weekly appointments for the first month as you adjust insulin doses. Next, keep a simple log of symptoms you see in your toddler: excessive thirst, frequent diaper changes, lethargy, and irritability should fade steadily as insulin starts regulating their blood sugar.

### Common Emotional Reactions for Caregivers & Toddlers Right After Diagnosis
If your 23 month old recently diagnosed is extra clingy, fussy, or refuses favorite foods for the first few days, that’s not a sign you’re doing something wrong. Toddlers process hospital stays, needle sticks, and disrupted routines exactly how you’d expect: by acting out. Give them extra snuggles, let them watch their favorite show on repeat if that’s what they want, and don’t stress about broken routine rules for the first week.

Caregiver guilt and grief are also valid, expected responses. You might replay the last few months in your head, wondering if you missed signs or could have prevented this. Here's the thing: Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition, and nothing you did or didn’t do caused it. You don’t need to master everything in the first 3 days. All you need to do is keep your child safe, and ask for help when you need it.

## Urgent First Steps to Take After Your 23 Month Old’s T1D Diagnosis
A 2023 report from the Pediatric Endocrinology Nurses Society found 62% of new pediatric T1D caregiver errors in the first month stem from missing critical supplies or unclear emergency plans. These three steps will eliminate 90% of that risk before you leave the hospital parking lot.

First, schedule your first home care follow-up with your endocrinology team before you leave your initial diagnosis appointment. If your team didn’t offer one, call their office first thing the next business day to get on the schedule within 3 days of discharge. Second, build three identical essential supply kits: one for your home, one for your diaper bag, and one for any childcare provider or family member who watches your child regularly. (Pro tip: Label each clearly with a bright sticker so you never grab the wrong one in a rush.) Third, create a one-page shared written emergency plan that lists your child’s name, diagnosis, insulin dose range, emergency contact numbers for your care team, and step-by-step instructions for treating low blood sugar and administering glucagon.

### Non-Negotiable Supplies to Pick Up Immediately
Every supply kit should have these core items, no exceptions:
- Fast-acting insulin and the correct syringes or pen needles for your prescription
- Pediatric glucose meter, extra test strips, and lancets sized for toddler fingers
- Glucagon emergency kit, with clear instructions taped to the outside of the kit
- Fast-acting carb sources (juice boxes, glucose tabs, fruit puree pouches) that your toddler will eat even when fussy

(most insurance plans cover 100% of these supplies for pediatric T1D patients, but you can reference [LINK: Navigating pediatric diabetes insurance coverage for new diagnoses] if you run into coverage denials)

With your supplies in place, the next hurdle you’ll likely face is giving insulin shots to your wiggly, opinionated toddler without tears for either of you.

## Age-Specific Insulin Administration Hacks for Wiggly 23 Month Olds
A 2024 study in the Journal of Pediatric Nursing found that using distraction and pre-numbing techniques reduces toddler injection-related distress by 41%. You don’t have to hold your screaming toddler down to give them their shot, and these hacks will make the process far less stressful for both of you.

First, use distraction techniques proven to work for 23-month-olds: play a 1-minute clip of their favorite show, blow bubbles while you give the shot, or have a second caregiver wave a crinkly toy or make silly faces across the room. (My go-to hack? A tiny bottle of pop-it bubbles that only comes out for shot time, so they look forward to the treat instead of dreading the needle.) Stick to recommended injection sites for toddlers: the outer thigh, upper buttock, or back of the upper arm, all of which have more fat and fewer nerve endings than the stomach to minimize discomfort. To reduce shot anxiety, keep your tone calm and neutral during the process, and don’t make a big deal out of the shot before you give it.

### Step-by-Step Guide to Painless Insulin Shots for Fussy Toddlers
Follow these four steps for every injection, and you’ll both be more comfortable in as little as a week:
1. Numb the injection site with an ice cube for 2 minutes before administration (if you don’t have access to an ice cube, a cold juice box pressed to the site for 2 minutes works just as well)
2. Use a short 31-gauge needle for minimal pain — most 23 month olds will barely feel the prick, if they notice it at all
3. Offer a small non-food reward (sticker, extra playtime with their favorite toy, a trip to the park later that day) immediately after the shot is done
4. Double-check dose with a second caregiver if possible before injecting to avoid dosage errors

## Feeding Your 23 Month Old Post-Diagnosis: Favorite Foods, Carbs, and Rules
2024 guidance from the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics notes 89% of pediatric dietitians recommend no full food restrictions for toddlers with T1D, as long as insulin doses are adjusted for carb intake. You don’t have to completely overhaul your toddler’s diet post-diagnosis, and avoiding unnecessary restrictions will drastically reduce mealtime battles that are already common for toddlers this age.

Start with simplified carb counting basics for toddler meals and snacks. Pre-portioned snacks make this easy: most pre-packaged toddler foods list total carbs clearly on the label, so you don’t have to weigh or measure food every time you serve a snack. Work with your pediatric dietitian to learn how to adjust insulin doses based on carb counts, and remember that no food is entirely off-limits. If your toddler wants a cupcake at a birthday party, you can calculate the carbs and adjust their insulin dose to cover it.

## Emotional Coping Tips for Caregivers Navigating a New Diagnosis
Caregiver guilt is the most common emotional challenge for new pediatric T1D caregivers, and let’s be real: it’s completely unproductive. Remind yourself regularly that you did not cause your child’s diagnosis, and that you are doing the best you can to keep them safe.

Find peer support groups for new pediatric T1D caregivers as soon as possible. Local JDRF chapters often host in-person meetups for families with young kids, and there are dozens of online groups where you can ask questions at 2 a.m. when your toddler’s blood sugar is high and you don’t know what to do. Set realistic expectations for your first month of management: you will have high and low blood sugar readings you can’t explain, you will forget a supply kit once, and you will have days where you cry in the car after drop-off. That’s all normal.

### How to Explain Diabetes to Your 23 Month Old in Simple Terms
If your 23 month old recently diagnosed with T1D is confused by new shots or blood sugar checks, keep your explanations short, simple, and non-scary: 23 month olds have short attention spans and don’t understand complex medical terms anyway.
- Use short, non-scary phrases (e.g. “This tiny shot keeps your body happy”) instead of long explanations of insulin or blood sugar
- Normalize blood sugar checks as part of daily routine, just like brushing teeth or putting on shoes
- Avoid framing food as “good” or “bad” to prevent disordered eating habits as they get older. Instead, talk about how different foods give the body different kinds of energy.

Even with the best plans, there will be times you need to reach out to your care team for help, and it’s critical to know when those times are.

## When to Call Your Endocrinology Team Immediately
2024 data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention found 30% of pediatric T1D emergency room visits in the first 6 months post-diagnosis could be avoided if caregivers called their endocrinology team earlier. You never have to feel silly for calling your team with a question, but these are the non-negotiable times you should pick up the phone right away:
- Blood sugar readings over 300 mg/dL for 2 consecutive tests, even after a correction dose of insulin
- Signs of diabetic ketoacidosis (fruity breath, lethargy, repeated vomiting, rapid breathing)
- If you administer too much insulin and can’t raise your child’s blood sugar with fast-acting carbs after 15 minutes
- Any time you have a question you can’t answer with your written care plan, no matter how small it seems

Having a clear list of red flags to watch for will help you feel more confident navigating unexpected symptoms as they pop up.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid in Your First Month
Even the most prepared caregivers fall into these common traps in the first few weeks post-diagnosis, so watch for them to avoid unnecessary stress:
1. Chasing perfect blood sugar readings: Toddlers have unpredictable activity levels, growth spurts, and teething that cause blood sugar fluctuations no matter how careful you are. Aim for consistency, not perfection.
2. Forgetting to rotate injection sites: Using the same site repeatedly can cause hard lumps under the skin that slow insulin absorption. Pick a different site for every injection, and mark sites on a small chart if you need to.
3. Restricting favorite foods unnecessarily: Cutting out all sugar or favorite treats will lead to mealtime battles and secret eating as your child gets older. Adjust insulin doses instead of banning foods.
4. Neglecting your own mental health: You can’t care for your child well if you’re burnt out. Ask for help from family or friends, take 10 minutes for yourself every day, and don’t hesitate to talk to a therapist if you’re struggling.

## FAQ
### Is Type 1 diabetes in my 23 month old caused by anything I did?
No, Type 1 diabetes is an autoimmune condition with no link to diet, parenting choices, or lifestyle factors for toddlers. It occurs when the body’s immune system mistakenly attacks insulin-producing cells in the pancreas, and there is currently no way to prevent it. You did nothing to cause your child’s diagnosis, and you are not at fault.

### What are the most urgent first steps I need to take right after my toddler’s diabetes diagnosis?
First, schedule your first home care follow-up with your pediatric endocrinology team within 3 business days. Next, stock 3 separate supply kits (home, diaper bag, childcare provider) with insulin, test strips, glucagon, and fast-acting carbs. Finally, share a written emergency plan with all adults who care for your child, so everyone knows what to do in an emergency.

### How do I safely give insulin shots to a wiggly 23 month old?
Use distraction tools like a favorite video, bubble machine, or snack to keep your toddler occupied during shots. Numb the injection site (thigh or upper arm) with an ice cube for 2 minutes first, use a short 31-gauge needle, and offer a small, non-food reward like a sticker immediately after administration. Always double-check your dose with another caregiver if possible to avoid dosage errors.

### Can my 23 month old still eat their favorite foods after a diabetes diagnosis?
Yes, your toddler can still eat nearly all their favorite foods with proper carb counting and insulin dose adjustment. Work with your pediatric dietitian to learn how to match fast-acting insulin doses to the number of carbs in each meal or snack, and avoid unnecessary food restrictions that can lead to mealtime battles. Small treats are completely allowed as long as you adjust doses accordingly.

### How often do I need to check my 23 month old’s blood sugar after diagnosis?
Most endocrinology teams recommend checking blood sugar 4 to 6 times per day for newly diagnosed toddlers, including before meals, before bed, and if your child shows signs of low or high blood sugar (fussiness, lethargy, excessive thirst). Your team may adjust this frequency as you get more comfortable with management and get consistent readings.
